Operating model specification  ·  Second Opinion Radiology UK

Who does what to a case, and in what order

The patient journey is built. This document specifies the two sides that make it actually deliver a report: the consultant who reads the scan and writes the opinion, and the operations team who match, check and release it. It defines one case lifecycle shared by all three roles, the handoffs between them, and the controls that stop a report reaching a patient before it should.

01

Three roles, one case

There is one case record. Each role sees a different projection of it, and each holds authority the other two do not have. Getting that boundary right is most of the design.

Patient

Owns: the images, the clinical question, and the decision to buy.

Can: submit, supply missing items, read the report, ask one follow-up, request a refund on a missed deadline.

Never sees: which consultants declined, how many times a draft was amended, internal QA notes, or the matching queue.

Consultant

Owns: the clinical content and the signature. This is the only role that can state a radiological opinion.

Can: accept or decline an offer, read the study, draft, amend, flag urgency, sign.

Cannot: release a report to a patient, change the price or deadline, see other consultants' cases, or edit anything after signing.

Operations (admin)

Owns: the pipeline — data completeness, matching, turnaround, delivery, money.

Can: chase missing items, offer and reassign, run non-clinical QA, release, refund, pause accounts.

Cannot: write or alter clinical content, write the patient-facing explanation, or sign anything.

The one control that matters most

The consultant signs; operations releases. These are two different acts by two different people and neither can perform the other's. Signing is a clinical and legal act tied to a named GMC registrant. Releasing is an operational act — confirming the document is complete, correctly attributed and safe to send. A single role doing both is how an unchecked or misattributed report reaches a patient.

02

Four stages the patient sees, fourteen the system tracks

The patient tracker already has four stages — Submitted, Being matched, Under expert review, Report ready — plus Action needed as an interrupt. Those four do not change. Everything the consultant and operations do happens inside them.

This is a deliberate asymmetry, not an oversight. A patient who has paid £299 and is frightened does not benefit from watching a case bounce between two consultants, or seeing a draft returned for amendment. They benefit from a stable picture and a deadline that holds. Internal churn is the business's problem to absorb.

There are exactly two reasons to break that rule, and both are obligations rather than status updates:

  • Action needed — the patient has to do something (a missing file, an unreadable upload, a consent). The clock pauses. They must be told.
  • A breached deadline — the promise was explicit and so is the failure. They are told, and refunded, without having to ask.

Say less, not more

"Being matched" covers an offer declined by two consultants and accepted by a third. "Under expert review" covers a draft returned once for an incomplete comparison. In both cases the honest patient-facing fact is unchanged: a consultant is being found, or a consultant is working on it. Exposing the churn would be transparency about process at the cost of confidence in outcome.

Why the offer is anonymised

At state 05 the consultant sees modality, body area, the clinical question, the deadline and the fee — not the patient's name or date of birth. Two reasons. It lets them decline cheaply without having touched identifiable data, which is the data-minimisation position under UK GDPR. And it makes the conflict-of-interest check meaningful: identity is revealed at acceptance, together with a direct question — have you reported for this patient before? — which they answer on the record before they can open the study.

The draft is a form, not a text box

The product's differentiator is a report with a finding category and a plain-English section, not a conventional radiology report. A free-text box produces conventional reports, because that is what radiologists write. Structuring the draft — with the category as a required choice and the patient-facing section as a required field — is what makes the promise on the website deliverable at volume rather than case by case.

The six finding categories already exist in the patient portal: confirms original finding, clarifies finding, additional finding, differing interpretation, no material difference, and change since your earlier scan. The consultant picks one. It is not a label operations applies afterwards — it is a clinical judgement, and it is the single most consequential word in the whole report.

Two rows that cannot move

The site tells patients the plain-English section is "written in plain English by [consultant], and approved by them before release". If operations drafts that section — even well, even from the consultant's own findings — the sentence is false and the service is mis-selling. The same applies to the finding category. Everything else in the pipeline can be optimised; these two cannot be reassigned.

06

Two operating models — the decision that shapes everything

A consultant radiologist has a reporting environment they already trust, with their hanging protocols, their voice recognition and their templates. Whether we ask them to leave it is the biggest product decision here.

Model A — authored in platform

The consultant drafts, categorises and signs inside the console. Images open in an embedded viewer or are downloaded alongside.

For: the structure is enforced, not hoped for. The plain-English section and the category are required fields. Versioning, audit and the clock are automatic. No file leaves the system.

Against: harder to recruit consultants — you are asking them to work somewhere unfamiliar, at speed. Needs a real viewer, which is a serious build.

Model B — reported outside, returned to operations

The consultant works in their own environment and returns a document. Operations uploads it against the case.

For: consultants work the way they already work. Far easier to recruit and to scale. No viewer to build on day one.

Against: what comes back is a conventional radiology report. The category and the patient-facing section are not in it, and ops cannot add them. Audit is weaker, the clock is manual, and identifiable documents travel by whatever channel the consultant chose.

The recommendation

Model B for the clinical body, Model A for the parts that make this product different. Let the consultant write findings and impression wherever they like and return it. But the finding category, the plain-English section and the signature are completed by the consultant in the console, against the uploaded document, before it can be signed. That is a short form — one dropdown, one paragraph, one button — not a reporting environment, so it does not fight their workflow. And it keeps the two fields that cannot be delegated with the person who has to own them.

This also answers the operational question directly: operations uploads the returned document, the system attaches it to the case as the clinical body, and the case cannot progress to Signed until the consultant has added the two fields and signed. Operations never types clinical content, and never signs.

What the return channel has to be

If documents come back from outside, the channel is part of the clinical system, not an afterthought. Email attachments to a shared inbox are the default answer and the wrong one: identifiable patient data, no audit trail, no guarantee of deletion. The minimum is an authenticated per-case upload link, valid for that case only, logged. This is the same pattern already specified for the GP/clinician invite in the patient flow — reuse it rather than inventing a second one.

07

Urgent findings

A second-opinion service will, sooner or later, be the first to see something that needs action today. The pipeline above takes hours and has a QA step in it. That is the wrong path for an urgent finding, and there has to be a defined other one.

StepWhoTarget
Consultant flags the case as urgent, with a short reasonConsultantImmediate
Operations alerted by phone, not by queueSystem< 1 min
Named duty person acknowledgesOperations< 15 min
Patient telephoned, with safety-netting advice and a named contactOperations< 1 hour
Report fast-tracked through QA and releasedOperationsSame day
Every step of the above recorded on the caseSystem—

Three things this flow needs that the product does not yet have

A phone number that is answered. The site already publishes 09:00–20:00 seven days. A 12-hour turnaround sold at 20:00 means an urgent finding can surface overnight. Either the hours cover the product, or the product does not sell turnarounds that finish outside them.

A named duty person. "Operations" cannot acknowledge anything. A rota with a name against each slot can.

A documented escalation if the patient cannot be reached. Voicemail, then the registered email, then — with consent captured at intake — their GP. That consent has to be asked for at intake, which means a change to the patient flow.

08

The clock, the buffer and the refund

The site sells three turnarounds and promises a full refund if one is missed. That promise is only safe if the consultant's deadline is earlier than the patient's, by enough to absorb a QA round.

ProductPatient promiseConsultant dueQA + second readRelease
Standard · £24948 h36 h8 h4 h
Next day · £29924 h16 h5 h3 h
Fastest · £34912 h7 h3 h2 h
  • The clock starts at payment, not at matching. That is already what the patient is told, and it means time lost finding a consultant is the business's loss, not theirs.
  • It pauses only in Action needed, and the pause is visible to the patient with the reason. Nothing else stops it — not a decline, not an amendment round.
  • The consultant sees their own deadline, not the patient's. Showing them 24 hours when the real internal target is 16 invites the whole buffer to be spent.
  • Breach is detected and acted on by the system, not claimed by the patient. The refund is issued and the patient is told. Making someone ask for a promised refund is how the promise stops being worth anything.

The 12-hour product is the one to watch

Seven hours of consultant time, on a case that may need a prior study chasing, matched to a subspecialist who has to be awake and free. It carries the thinnest buffer and the highest refund exposure. Worth modelling how many of these can be sold per day against the panel's real availability before it is marketed as broadly as the other two.

Standing note on the prototype

Everything in the built prototype is demonstration content. The eleven consultants are real named people whose roles, hospitals and qualifications have not been verified, and every GMC number is the placeholder 7012345. None of this is safe to show outside the project until the client has confirmed it.
